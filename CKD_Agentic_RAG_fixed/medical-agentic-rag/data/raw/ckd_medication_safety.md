# Medication safety concepts in Chronic Kidney Disease

> **DEMO CONTENT — NOT FOR CLINICAL USE.** Written to exercise retrieval code.
> This is not a validated interaction database and contains no numeric doses.
> Any decision about combining or changing medicines belongs to a clinician or
> pharmacist who knows the individual.

## Why combinations matter more in CKD

General references describe the kidney as having limited reserve in CKD. Several
medicines that are individually tolerated are described as acting on the same
regulatory mechanisms — blood flow into and out of the filtering units, circulating
volume, and potassium handling. When such medicines are combined, the described
concern is usually additive: each one removes part of the kidney's capacity to
compensate.

## ACE inhibitor and NSAID

Combining an ACE inhibitor such as lisinopril with an NSAID such as ibuprofen is
commonly described as a concern in CKD. The mechanism generally given is that the
two act on opposite sides of the filtering unit: the ACE inhibitor is described as
reducing pressure on the outgoing side, while the NSAID is described as reducing
prostaglandin-supported flow on the incoming side. General references describe the
combination as associated with reduced kidney perfusion, a rise in potassium, and
an increased chance of acute deterioration, particularly where a person is also
volume-depleted.

## ARB and NSAID

The same concern is described for an ARB such as losartan combined with an NSAID.
Because ARBs and ACE inhibitors act on the same renin-angiotensin system, general
references treat the NSAID concern as applying to both classes rather than to one.

## The "triple whammy"

General references use the informal term **triple whammy** for the combination of
three medicine groups taken together:

1. an ACE inhibitor **or** an ARB, plus
2. a diuretic, plus
3. an NSAID

The described reasoning combines the individual effects. The diuretic is described
as reducing circulating volume; the ACE inhibitor or ARB is described as reducing
the kidney's ability to maintain filtration pressure when volume falls; and the NSAID
is described as removing the prostaglandin-mediated compensation that would otherwise
preserve blood flow into the filtering unit. General references describe the risk of
acute kidney injury as higher with all three together than with any pair, and describe
the risk as further increased during intercurrent illness involving dehydration.

The individual pairwise relationships within this combination — ACE inhibitor with
NSAID, ARB with NSAID, and a renin-angiotensin medicine with a diuretic — are each
described as relevant in their own right, which is why they are recorded separately
in this prototype's structured store.

General references commonly discuss "sick day" guidance in this context: the idea that
a clinician may give a person advance instructions about temporarily pausing certain
medicines during an acute illness with dehydration. Any such plan is individualised and
must come from the treating clinician; this demo corpus does not provide one.

## Dual renin-angiotensin system blockade

Combining an ACE inhibitor with an ARB — for example lisinopril with losartan — is
described in general references as dual blockade of the renin-angiotensin system.
The described finding is that the combination has generally not shown a benefit that
outweighs its harms, with increased rates of high potassium, low blood pressure and
acute kidney injury described relative to using a single agent. General references
therefore describe routine combined use as something to avoid outside specific
specialist circumstances.

## Anticoagulants and NSAIDs

Combining an anticoagulant such as warfarin with an NSAID such as ibuprofen is widely
described as increasing bleeding risk relative to either agent used alone. The concern
is usually framed as additive: reduced clotting capacity from the anticoagulant, plus
described effects of the NSAID on platelets and on the gastric mucosa. General
patient-facing material typically advises seeking prompt medical attention for signs of
significant bleeding.

## General principles discussed for medicines in CKD

Themes that recur across general CKD references include:

- Reviewing whether a medicine's handling changes as kidney function declines.
- Being cautious with medicines available without prescription, NSAIDs in particular.
- Considering potassium when several medicines that raise it are used together.
- Reviewing medicines during acute illness, dehydration, and around contrast procedures.
- Keeping an up-to-date list of all medicines, including over-the-counter products and
  supplements, so a clinician or pharmacist can review the whole picture.

None of this replaces individualised advice. Consult a clinician or pharmacist.
