# Chronic Kidney Disease — overview and staging

> **DEMO CONTENT — NOT FOR CLINICAL USE.** Written to exercise retrieval code.
> Not validated clinical guidance. Consult a clinician for any decision about
> kidney care.

## What Chronic Kidney Disease is

Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) is the general term for a long-standing reduction in
kidney function, or for kidney damage that persists over time. General references
describe it as a condition defined by *persistence*: findings must be present for
an extended period rather than reflecting a single abnormal result or a short-lived
illness. A temporary fall in kidney function during an acute illness is usually
described separately as acute kidney injury.

The kidneys are generally described as filtering waste products from the blood,
regulating fluid balance, helping control blood pressure, contributing to
electrolyte balance, and supporting bone and red blood cell health. When function
declines, general references note that several of these roles may be affected at
once, which is why CKD is often discussed alongside blood pressure, anaemia,
electrolyte and bone-health considerations.

## eGFR and staging concepts

CKD staging is conventionally described as being based on the **estimated
glomerular filtration rate (eGFR)**, which is an estimate of how much blood the
kidneys filter over time. eGFR is generally calculated from a blood creatinine
measurement together with factors such as age and sex, using a published
estimating equation.

General references describe eGFR-based categories, usually labelled G1 to G5, which
run from normal or high filtration at one end through to kidney failure at the
other. The categories are conventionally summarised as:

- **G1** — normal or high eGFR, with other evidence of kidney damage present
- **G2** — mildly reduced eGFR, with other evidence of kidney damage present
- **G3a** — mildly to moderately reduced eGFR
- **G3b** — moderately to severely reduced eGFR
- **G4** — severely reduced eGFR
- **G5** — kidney failure

An important point made in general references is that G1 and G2 are not defined by
eGFR alone. A normal filtration estimate does not by itself exclude CKD; a marker
of kidney damage must also be present for those categories to apply.

## Albuminuria

Alongside eGFR, general references describe **albuminuria** — albumin appearing in
the urine — as a marker of kidney damage and as a second axis used to describe CKD.
It is commonly assessed using a urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio. Albuminuria
categories are conventionally labelled A1 to A3, running from normal or mildly
increased albumin excretion through to severely increased excretion.

Because eGFR and albuminuria are described as two separate axes, general references
often present CKD as a grid rather than a single number: a person's description
combines a G category with an A category. Increasing albuminuria is generally
described as associated with a less favourable outlook at any given eGFR category.

## Causes and risk factors

General references most commonly list diabetes mellitus and long-standing high
blood pressure as the leading causes of CKD. Other causes and contributing factors
frequently described include glomerular diseases, polycystic kidney disease and
other inherited conditions, recurrent urinary obstruction, repeated episodes of
acute kidney injury, and long-term exposure to nephrotoxic substances.

Risk factors commonly discussed include older age, a family history of kidney
disease, cardiovascular disease, obesity, and smoking. CKD is often described as
being without obvious symptoms in its earlier stages, which is why general
references discuss detection through testing in people considered at higher risk
rather than through symptoms alone.

## Progression concepts

Progression is generally described as the gradual decline of eGFR over time,
though the rate is said to vary widely between individuals and is not uniform.
Factors commonly described as associated with faster progression include higher
levels of albuminuria, poorly controlled blood pressure, poorly controlled blood
glucose in diabetes, and repeated acute kidney injury episodes.

General references typically frame CKD management around slowing progression and
managing associated problems, rather than around reversing established damage.
Blood pressure control, management of the underlying cause, and avoidance of
avoidable kidney insults are the themes most often discussed. The specific
approach for any individual is a clinical decision.
