# Medicines commonly discussed in Chronic Kidney Disease

> **DEMO CONTENT — NOT FOR CLINICAL USE.** Written to exercise retrieval code.
> No numeric doses appear anywhere in this document, deliberately. Decisions about
> starting, stopping or changing any medicine belong to a qualified prescriber.

## ACE inhibitors

Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors, such as lisinopril and ramipril,
are described in general references as blood-pressure-lowering medicines that act
on the renin-angiotensin system. In the context of CKD they are commonly discussed
for two reasons: blood pressure control, and a described effect on reducing
albuminuria, particularly where albuminuria is present.

General references note that ACE inhibitors are often accompanied by monitoring of
kidney function and serum potassium after starting or after a change, because they
are described as capable of causing a rise in potassium and a change in measured
kidney function. A small early change in creatinine is commonly described as
expected, while a larger change prompts clinical review. Cough is the side effect
most frequently mentioned. Consult a clinician about whether this class is
appropriate in any individual situation.

## ARBs

Angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), such as losartan and valsartan, act on the
same renin-angiotensin system as ACE inhibitors but at a different point. General
references describe them as an alternative where an ACE inhibitor is not tolerated,
cough being the commonly cited reason for switching.

The monitoring considerations described for ARBs broadly mirror those for ACE
inhibitors: kidney function and potassium are generally discussed as things a
clinician follows. Because the two classes act on the same system, general
references treat combining them as a specific concern rather than as additive
benefit; this is discussed further in the medication safety note.

## SGLT2 inhibitors

Sodium-glucose co-transporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, such as dapagliflozin and
empagliflozin, were originally introduced as glucose-lowering medicines for type 2
diabetes. General references now discuss them in CKD more broadly, describing an
observed benefit on kidney outcomes that is not limited to their effect on blood
glucose, and that has been described in people both with and without diabetes.

A point frequently made is that a temporary reduction in eGFR is commonly described
after starting an SGLT2 inhibitor, and that this early dip is generally discussed as
expected rather than as a sign of harm. Side effects commonly mentioned include
genital and urinary tract infections, and volume-related effects. General references
also note that eligibility is usually described with reference to a person's level
of kidney function. Whether this class is suitable for anyone in particular is a
clinical decision.

## Loop diuretics

Loop diuretics, such as furosemide, are described as medicines that increase urine
output by acting on the loop of Henle. In CKD they are commonly discussed for fluid
overload and for oedema, and general references note that they tend to remain
effective at lower levels of kidney function where some other diuretic classes are
described as less so.

Considerations commonly described include dehydration and volume depletion,
electrolyte disturbance, and low blood pressure. Because volume depletion is itself
described as a stress on the kidneys, general references discuss diuretics as
requiring monitoring rather than as uniformly protective.

## NSAIDs and why they matter in CKD

Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), such as ibuprofen, naproxen and
diclofenac, are used for pain, fever and inflammation, and many are available
without prescription. They are among the medicines most consistently flagged in
general CKD references.

The reason usually given is that NSAIDs are described as reducing prostaglandin
production. Prostaglandins are described as helping maintain blood flow within the
kidney, and that supporting role is described as more important when the kidney is
already under strain — which includes CKD, dehydration and older age. General
references therefore describe NSAIDs as capable of reducing kidney perfusion,
worsening fluid retention, raising blood pressure, and contributing to acute
deterioration in people with existing kidney disease.

NSAIDs are commonly described as medicines to discuss with a clinician or pharmacist
before use in CKD, including over-the-counter products and combination remedies that
may contain an NSAID without it being obvious from the packaging.

## Metformin and CKD

Metformin is an oral biguanide used in the management of type 2 diabetes. It is
relevant to CKD because it is described as being cleared by the kidneys, so its
handling changes as kidney function declines.

General references describe metformin as requiring review against a person's level
of kidney function, with dose adjustment described at reduced function and with use
described as not recommended below a certain level of function. The concern usually
cited is accumulation and an associated risk of lactic acidosis, which is described
as rare but serious. References also commonly describe temporarily withholding
metformin during acute illness involving dehydration, vomiting or diarrhoea, and
around procedures using iodinated contrast.

**This demo corpus stores no numeric dosing values for metformin or any other
medicine.** Any dose adjustment for kidney function is an individualised clinical
decision; consult the prescribing clinician and the official product label.

## Warfarin

Warfarin is an oral vitamin K antagonist anticoagulant with a narrow therapeutic
index, conventionally monitored using the international normalised ratio (INR) under
clinician supervision. It appears in this CKD-focused corpus because anticoagulation
and bleeding risk are commonly discussed in kidney disease, and because it
illustrates an interaction with NSAIDs that the structured store also records.

General references describe many drugs, foods and illnesses as capable of altering
warfarin's effect, and describe bleeding risk as a central consideration. Decisions
about anticoagulation belong to the treating clinician.
