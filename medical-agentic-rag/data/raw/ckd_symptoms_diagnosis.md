# CKD symptoms, causes and kidney function tests

> **DEMO CONTENT — NOT FOR CLINICAL USE.** Written to exercise retrieval code.
> Not validated clinical guidance. Symptoms overlap with many other conditions and
> cannot be used to diagnose kidney disease. Consult a clinician.

## Common symptoms of chronic kidney disease

A point made consistently in general references is that chronic kidney disease is
frequently **asymptomatic in its early stages**. Many people with reduced kidney
function feel entirely well, which is why CKD is usually described as detected
through blood and urine testing rather than through how a person feels.

When symptoms of chronic kidney disease do appear, they are generally described as
developing gradually and as becoming more noticeable at more advanced stages. The
symptoms most commonly listed in general patient-facing references include:

- **Tiredness and low energy** — fatigue is among the most frequently reported symptoms.
- **Swelling (oedema)** — typically described in the ankles, feet, legs or around the eyes, reflecting fluid retention.
- **Changes in urination** — passing urine more often, particularly at night, or passing less urine than usual; urine that appears foamy or frothy is described in connection with protein loss.
- **Poor appetite, nausea and an unpleasant taste** — commonly described as appearing at more advanced stages.
- **Itchy skin (pruritus)** — associated with the build-up of waste products.
- **Muscle cramps** — often discussed alongside disturbances of electrolytes such as calcium and phosphate.
- **Shortness of breath** — described in relation to fluid accumulation or to anaemia.
- **Difficulty concentrating and disturbed sleep.**
- **Headaches and raised blood pressure**, which is described both as a cause and as a consequence of kidney disease.

General references emphasise two cautions about these symptoms. First, they are
**non-specific**: every one of them occurs in many conditions unrelated to the
kidneys. Second, their absence does not rule CKD out, because substantial loss of
kidney function can occur before any symptom appears. This is why testing, not
symptom checking, is described as the basis for detection.

## Causes and risk factors

The two causes listed most often as leading to chronic kidney disease are
**diabetes mellitus** and **long-standing high blood pressure (hypertension)**.
Other causes commonly described include:

- **Glomerular diseases**, such as glomerulonephritis, affecting the filtering units.
- **Polycystic kidney disease** and other inherited kidney conditions.
- **Recurrent or prolonged urinary tract obstruction**, including from an enlarged prostate or kidney stones.
- **Repeated episodes of acute kidney injury**, which may leave lasting reduction in function.
- **Long-term exposure to nephrotoxic substances**, including regular heavy use of NSAIDs.
- **Recurrent kidney infection (pyelonephritis)** and vesicoureteral reflux.

Risk factors frequently discussed alongside these causes include older age, a family
history of kidney disease, cardiovascular disease, obesity, and smoking.

## Tests used to evaluate kidney function

Assessment of kidney function is generally described as resting on a small set of
blood and urine tests, usually interpreted together rather than in isolation.

### Blood tests

- **Serum creatinine** — a waste product of muscle metabolism cleared by the kidneys. A rise is described as indicating reduced clearance.
- **Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR)** — calculated from serum creatinine together with factors such as age and sex, using a published estimating equation. eGFR is the measure CKD staging categories (G1 to G5) are based on.
- **Cystatin C** — described as an alternative or confirmatory marker where creatinine-based estimates may be unreliable.
- **Urea (BUN)** and **electrolytes**, including potassium, sodium, bicarbonate, calcium and phosphate.

### Urine tests

- **Urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (uACR)** — the standard way albuminuria is quantified, and the basis of the A1 to A3 albuminuria categories.
- **Urine protein-to-creatinine ratio (uPCR)** — used in some settings as an alternative measure of protein loss.
- **Urinalysis / urine dipstick** — screening for protein, blood, glucose and signs of infection; microscopy may be used to look for cells and casts.

### Imaging and other investigations

- **Kidney ultrasound** — commonly described for assessing kidney size and structure, and for detecting obstruction or cysts.
- **Kidney biopsy** — described as reserved for cases where the underlying cause remains unclear and a tissue diagnosis would change management.

### Why two measures are used together

General references stress that eGFR and albuminuria describe **different things**:
eGFR estimates how much the kidneys filter, while albuminuria indicates damage to
the filtering barrier. A person can have significant albuminuria with a normal eGFR.
Because a single abnormal result may reflect a temporary illness, dehydration or
another transient cause, CKD is defined by findings that **persist over time**, so
repeat testing is described as necessary before the label is applied.

## Limitations of this demo content

This document contains no numeric thresholds, no reference ranges, no diagnostic
criteria and no drug doses. It must not be used to interpret anyone's test results
or to decide whether a person has kidney disease. Consult a clinician.
